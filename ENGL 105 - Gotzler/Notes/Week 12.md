### Computing Criticism Overview

**Main Argument:**

Rather than treating computational methods as imperfect tools that approximate "real" literary phenomena (like authorship, character, or theme), Algee-Hewitt argues we should take computational results seriously as alternative models that can challenge and expand our traditional humanities concepts.

**Key Examples:**

1. **Authorship**: Stylometric analysis (studying word frequency patterns) splits Sue Grafton's detective novels into two distinct clusters—before and after her first bestseller. Rather than viewing this as an error, it reveals how publishing success fundamentally changed her writing in ways traditional criticism misses.
2. **Character**: Natural language processing that tracks character references in Jane Austen's _Pride and Prejudice_ sometimes "confuses" Elizabeth Bennet and Mr. Darcy, assigning female pronouns to Darcy. This isn't a mistake—it captures moments where Elizabeth's thoughts about Darcy blend with his actual character, revealing the fluid boundaries between characters.
3. **Topic Modeling**: When algorithms group novels by word patterns, they create unexpected clusters (sea tales, college novels, mysteries) that don't fit neatly into traditional categories like "theme" or "genre," revealing how texts actually organize themselves.

**Core Insight:**

Both humanities theories and computational models are approximations of complex texts. By treating computational results as legitimate interpretive objects rather than just flawed tools, we can discover new ways of understanding literature.
