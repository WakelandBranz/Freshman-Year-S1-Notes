> **If P, then Q** (P → Q)

is **considered true** when **P is false**, even if **Q is true**.

---

### 🔸 First, Remember: A Conditional is a Promise

Think of the conditional **P → Q** like someone making a **promise**:

> "If P happens, then Q will happen."

---

### 🔸 Scenario: P is False, Q is True

Now imagine this:

> **P**: It rains. (**False** — it didn’t rain)  
> **Q**: I brought an umbrella. (**True** — I did)

So the full statement is:

> "If it rains, then I will bring an umbrella."

But it **didn't rain**, and I **still brought** the umbrella.

Did I break the promise? **No** — because the condition (rain) never even happened. So I can’t be blamed or called a liar.

So we say:

> The conditional statement is **still true**.

---

### 🔸 Why This Works in Logic

In formal logic, we care about **consistency** and **rules**, not emotions or intentions.

The rule is:

> A conditional only **fails** (is false) when the **"if" part is true**, and the **"then" part is false**.

So in this case:

- **P is false** → we never required anything of Q.
    
- So no contradiction, no broken rule → **it's true** by default.
    

---

### 🔸 Everyday Analogy

Imagine someone says:

> "If I win the lottery, I’ll buy you a car."

If they **don’t win the lottery** and **still buy you a car**, they’ve gone above and beyond — they **didn’t break the promise**. The promise only breaks if they **win the lottery** and **don’t** buy the car.

---

### 🔸 TL;DR

- When **P is false**, the conditional doesn’t require anything from Q.
    
- Therefore, **P → Q** is **true** in that case — because the "if" never happened, so the "then" isn’t tested.
