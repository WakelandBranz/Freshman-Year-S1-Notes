
### Definitions
> Argument
> 	A sequence of propositions, which we call premises, and the last proposition in the sequence is called the ***conclusion***.

##### We say an argument is valid if the truth of the premises implies the truth of the conclusion. (As in, the conclusion ===cannot=== be false if the premises are true)

- *If it is sunny, I will go play football*.
	- Premises 1
- It is sunny! I will play football.
	- *It is sunny* - premises 2
	- ***Conclusion - I will play football***

##### You can have a valid argument but your premises be false

### ===THERE IS NO DIFFERENCE BETWEEN AN ARGUMENT AND A CONDITIONAL STATEMENT===

 - Validity of an argument follows the truth table of a conditional (it seems?)

> Argument form - The rewriting of an argument using propositional logic (i.e. p's, q's, n's, v's, ...)
> 	We say an argument form is ===valid=== if the truth of the premises is independent of the actual statements substituted in the form. (i.e. the implication/conditional formed by premises -> conclusion, is a tautology)

- Example: "If you have the current password, then you can log in." | p -> q
	- "You have the correct password." | p
	- "You can log in." | q
- Example: "If you have access to WiFi, then you can change your grade."
	- "You have access to wifi."
	- "You can change your grade."


> The argument is all 3 lines. The first two are the premises, the last line is the conclusion.
> 	p -> q | *This line and the two below are the **argument form***
> 	p | ((p -> q) and p) -> q is a tautology
> 	q | This is a ===valid=== argument
> 	The above argument form is known as *Modus Ponens* ("mode that affirms")

---

### TL;DR (the easiest way)

- **Argument = premises + conclusion** (a short chain of statements).
- **Valid** = “If the premises are true, the conclusion must be true.”
- **Truth of premises** doesn’t matter for validity; it matters for _soundness_.
- Think of it like a **conditional** (“If P then Q”) plus the fact that **P actually happens**, letting you safely conclude **Q**.

---

The **form** of the argument is valid (it’s a classic _modus ponens_), but the **conclusion can still be false** if the first premise (“If you have Wi‑Fi, then you can change your grade”) is itself false.

- **Valid** = _If the premises were true, the conclusion could not be false._
- **Sound** = _The premises are actually true **and** the argument is valid._

In your example:

1. Premise 1: “If you have Wi‑Fi, then you can change your grade.” – this statement may be **false** (maybe Wi‑Fi doesn’t give you that ability).
2. Premise 2: “You have Wi‑Fi.” – true.

Because Premise 1 is false, the whole argument is **unsound**; the conclusion (“You can change your grade”) need not follow in reality, even though the logical pattern is correct. So the conclusion can be invalid in practice, but the inference rule itself remains valid.
